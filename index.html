What this does, and what it can't

What it does

You give it a sound. It computes the phase-inverted copy — a waveform where every peak becomes a trough and every trough a peak. If you play both at once through the same output, they sum to zero. That's active noise cancellation, in its purest form.

What it can't do

It cannot cancel live environmental noise in real time. To cancel a sound as it happens, the anti-noise has to reach your ear within about 0.05 ms of the noise. A browser runs at 10–50 ms, and Bluetooth earbuds add another 150–300 ms. That is thousands of times too slow. Real ANC chips in your earbuds do this in dedicated silicon sitting centimetres from your eardrum.

So what is it actually good for?

  • Understanding cancellation. Upload any clip, hit Prove cancellation, and hear what phase inversion really sounds like. That alone is worth trying.
  • Generating anti-noise WAVs. Export the inverted file and use it in a DAW, a looping player, or an audio tool where you can sync it to a source.
  • Cancelling steady loops. If you have a recording of a fan, a hum, a drone — the inverted file, played from the same playback device at the same moment the original starts, will partially cancel it.

How to get the best cancellation

  1. Upload a clean recording of the sound you want to kill — as little extra noise as possible.
  2. Use Rumble preset if it's a fan, AC, engine, or hum. Leave Full-band only for wideband material.
  3. Nudge Depth so the anti-noise matches the source volume — usually 100%.
  4. Nudge Time offset in tiny steps. If cancellation is perfect, Prove cancellation yields near-silence. If it yields a hollow doubling, you're a fraction of a millisecond off.
  5. Export the WAV. Use it wherever you can play it back sample-aligned to the source.

Why a perfect upload still won't silence your room

Because the recording captured the sound at a previous moment in a particular place. By the time you play the inverse, the room has moved on. Waves have already hit your ear. Physics wins.
